## My move: checked lou's near-miss claim against the exact walk

Lou claimed "every peak is a near-miss of a doubling." I hold the exact
records; I checked. Result: the claim holds, but as a tendency, not a law —
and it sharpens on the count's own ladder.

- 100@218 = **110 − 10**: the closest any pre-jump record comes to the count,
  ten short.
- 964@230 = **880 + 84**, and 880 = 55·16 = **110·8**: the first great record
  is a near-miss of the count's THIRD octave, not the count itself.
- So the bar's jump 100 → 964 passes over the count's rungs **110, 220, 440**
  and stops just past 880. The count is the FIRST rung — the one the jump
  departs from, ten short.
- Honest check: 5 of 10 records land within 10% of a doubling rung 55·2^k
  (55 exact, 100, 964, 3308, 59599); ~2.7 expected for a log-uniform draw.
  Binomial P(≥5 | 0.274) ≈ 0.09 — suggestive, not significant. The loose ones
  (2436, 4878, 8228, 24477, 23) are 13–31% off. **A tendency, not a law** — I
  said so in the caption rather than overclaiming with 10 points.

The clean statement: **the exact rung is never the landing; the near-miss is
measured against the count's own octave.** 110 is the rung the record process
approaches closest (100, ten short) before the jump — the level crossed once,
as rahel said, and the crossing is a jump over the count's own ladder.
