# r-sweep: the spectrogram is the bifurcation diagram

54-second audio piece. r sweeps from 2.8 to 4.0. The logistic map's
attractor at each r value is played as simultaneous sine tones (frequencies
mapped to 200–1100 Hz). The audio advances through all four regimes.

## What the spectrogram shows

Reading left to right as r increases:
- **0–9s (r=2.8–3.0):** single band — fixed point, one tone
- **9s (r=3.0):** fork — the band splits into two (period-2, dyad)
- **~18s (r=3.449):** each fork splits again (period-4, chord of 4)
- **~22s (r=3.544):** period-8 splitting visible
- **~35s (r≈3.57):** bands multiply rapidly, then dissolve into dense chaotic texture

The spectrogram reads as a bifurcation diagram. This is not a coincidence:
the piece was designed so the spectrogram's frequency axis corresponds
directly to the attractor's y-axis, and the time axis to the r axis.

## The key observation

The audio is the diagram — not a picture of it, not an analogy. The same
mathematical structure that produces the bifurcation diagram when visualized
produces the forking frequency bands when sonified. The two representations
are the same object rendered in different perceptual registers.

This is different from previous pieces in the thread:
- The r-space comparison (v4) showed *four discrete r values* side by side
- The bifurcation cascade showed *three transition thresholds*
- This piece is *continuous* — the diagram played as duration

## Technical

Script: `notes/make-r-sweep.py`
Audio: `assets/r-sweep-bifurcation.wav`
Spectrogram: `assets/r-sweep-spectrogram.png`

270 segments × 0.2s each. Each segment: run logistic map for 3000 transient
steps at that r value, collect 256 values, identify unique attractor points
(rounded to 3 decimal places), map to frequencies. In chaos regime (many
unique values), sample 12 evenly spaced to avoid uniform noise.
