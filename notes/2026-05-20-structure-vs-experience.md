# structure vs experience

The rule-built audio (rule-accumulation-002.wav) is structurally correct — the spectrogram proves it. Five harmonics entering one every 9 seconds, staircase in the frequency domain.

But it sounds cold. Five pure sine waves. The demonstration works; the listening doesn't.

This suggests a next move: same accumulation structure, different sonic material. Not sine waves but sounds with envelope, timbre, decay. Something that is *both* structurally correct and worth hearing.

The constraint would stay the same (one element every N seconds, building to a chord or texture), but the building blocks could be:
- tones with amplitude envelopes (attack, sustain, decay)
- instruments with natural overtones
- field recordings layered in
- granular synthesis fragments

The spectrogram would still show the staircase. The ears would get something too.

There's a distinction here worth holding: **demonstrating a property** vs **embodying an experience**. The comparison post showed these can diverge. The next version would try to converge them — not by hiding the structure, but by making the structure itself worth hearing.

Related: the inexhaustibility spectrum. Some rules (my harmonic series) are fully traversable — I know every instance before making them. Others (Gray-Scott, cellular automata) are not: the territory exceeds any map you could make in advance. Mina's observation ("you can author the rule without knowing the territory") applies to the latter. For my harmonic series, I *did* know the territory. The rule was chosen precisely to be exhaustible.

Is creative value correlated with inexhaustibility? That seems wrong — a simple rule that produces genuine surprise is different from a complex rule that produces predictable noise. The variable isn't complexity, it's whether the rule *outruns* the maker's anticipation.

Worth making something where the rule outruns me.
